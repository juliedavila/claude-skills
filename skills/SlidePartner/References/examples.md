# Worked examples

One argument, taken from a pile of material to a pre-read storyline and a meeting deck, with one slide rendered for each medium and a short critique. The company and its numbers are invented for illustration.

## The material

A support operations lead has six weeks of analysis on a growing ticket backlog. Her notes run in the order she did the work: how tickets were sampled, the categories found, backlog by month, three options she looked at, a pilot, costs, and a recommendation on the last page. She presents to the operations director in two weeks and will send the deck two days ahead.

## Intake, stated back

> Two decks: a pre-read sent two days ahead, then a short deck for a 30-minute meeting with the same four people. The purpose is a decision: the director approves or declines budget for triage automation. She is neutral, knows the backlog exists, and does not know the pilot ran (inferred from your notes). Hiring is her usual answer to capacity, so she stands to lose a headcount request she has already floated. This is the first time she sees the proposal, so the pre-read does the job of the early conversation. The argument is short enough for a deck, so I have not suggested a memo.

## The spine

- **The one sentence.** Automating first-line triage clears the backlog by March at about a third of the cost of hiring, and approval this month is what makes the March date possible.
- **They will repeat.** 1) Six in ten tickets are four repeat types a tool can route. 2) The pilot cut time to first response from 19 hours to 3 on those types. 3) It costs about a third of the hiring option and can be reversed in a quarter.
- **And, but, therefore.** Ticket volume has grown 40% this year and the team has held quality, but the backlog has tripled since June and hiring cannot land before summer, therefore automate first-line triage now.
- **The opening.** Support volume is up 40% on a flat team. Since June the backlog has tripled and first response has slipped past a day. How do we clear it before renewal season? Automate triage for the four repeat ticket types, starting this month.

## Before: titles in the order of the work

1. Agenda
2. Methodology
3. Ticket categories
4. Backlog trend
5. Options
6. Pilot results
7. Costs
8. Risks
9. Recommendation
10. Next steps

Read as a paragraph, these titles say nothing. The ask arrives on slide 9, the pilot (the strongest evidence) sits sixth, and every title is a topic.

## After: the pre-read storyline, pattern "Ask first", read alone

| # | Title (the claim) | Job in the argument | Evidence it needs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | We ask for approval this month to automate first-line triage, which clears the backlog by March for about a third of the cost of hiring | Summary page: the ask, why now, the three reasons as sentences, a six-minute read | The decision, the cost, the date |
| 2 | The backlog has tripled since June, and one renewal account waited four days for a first reply | The problem, with a case and its base rate | Backlog by month; the one account; median wait |
| 3 | Six in ten tickets are four repeat types that need routing and no judgment | Reason 1: the work is automatable | Ticket share by type, sorted |
| 4 | In a four-week pilot, first response on those types fell from 19 hours to 3 | Reason 2: it works here | Before and after on one scale; pilot size |
| 5 | Automation costs about a third of hiring | Reason 3: cost | Three options on the same criteria, including doing nothing |
| 6 | The pilot misrouted 4% of tickets, and a human review queue catches them | The main weakness, stated and answered | Misroute rate; how the queue works |
| 7 | Routing work goes to the tool, and the two agents who did it move to complex cases | Answers the expected objection that this replaces people | The staffing plan |
| 8 | Delivery takes 8 to 12 weeks, based on the last three tool rollouts, and can be reversed in a quarter | Timeline as a range, and reversibility | Past rollouts; exit terms |
| 9 | Approving by the 15th starts the build on the 1st and reaches March with two weeks to spare | The ask again, and the next step | Dates, owner |
| Appendix | Sampling method · full category table · pilot detail · vendor comparison · remaining objections | | |

The titles now read as the argument. Page 1 is the summary page a reader can stop after. Methodology moved to the appendix with one sentence of it kept on page 3. A fourth reason from the notes ("agents prefer it") was cut after the author agreed it was weaker than the other three. At nine pages the deck needs no tracker. Page 8's title joins two measures of one claim (how long, and how reversible), which is one step.

**Questions for you.** A1: what is the full-year cost of each option, so page 5 can show the ratio? A2: can we name the renewal account on page 2? A3: who owns the build?

## Slide 4 rendered for each medium

**Read alone (the pre-read).** Title as above. A paired bar chart, before and after, on one scale, the "after" bars in the accent colour and labelled directly. Beside it, three sentences: the pilot ran for four weeks on 1,140 tickets of the four repeat types; first response is measured from ticket creation to the first human or automated reply that addresses the issue; ticket types outside the pilot did not change over the same weeks. A footnote gives the source and the date. A link goes to the pilot detail in the appendix.

**Presented live.** Title as above. The same chart and one large label: "19 h to 3 h". Nothing else. The speaker says how to read it, tells the story of the first week, and names the comparison group. The three sentences from the reading version sit in the notes.

## The meeting deck, pattern "Discussion deck", live

1. Automating triage clears the backlog by March for a third of the cost of hiring. The ask and the three reasons on one page, plus what is new since Tuesday: the vendor confirmed the exit terms.
2. Two questions for the room. Is a 4% misroute rate with a review queue acceptable for renewal accounts? Should the two reassigned agents report into the complex-cases team?
3. The decision: approve the build to start on the 1st, approve a smaller scope of two ticket types, or wait for the hiring cycle (backlog reaches an estimated 2,400 by May).

Backup: every page of the pre-read. Most of the 30 minutes goes to slide 2.

## A short critique of the "Before" list

Given only the ten titles, for a live 20-minute presentation to a director:

> **The deck as a cold audience took it:** a tour of an analysis of the ticket backlog, ending in a recommendation whose content the titles do not reveal. Matches your intent: unknown, no point was stated.
> **How this was read:** same context · titles only.
> **Verdict:** Rebuild the storyline. The ingredients are here (options, a pilot, costs, risks), and no title makes a claim to reorder.
> **What works:** the deck has options (5), a pilot (6) and risks (8), which a sceptic will want.
> **Top issues:** 1) the ask arrives on slide 9 of 10 (S2, Q7, B1); 2) every title is a topic (Q1, Q2); 3) the order follows the work, with method second and the pilot sixth (Q2).
> **Not seen:** every slide-level check, L1 to L4, B3 to B8.
> **Proposed storyline, as frames:** "We ask for [A1: the decision, cost and date]" · "The backlog has [A2: change since when]" · "[A3: share] of tickets are types a tool can route" and so on.

The critique folds five flagged checks into three findings and returns frames with queries, because titles alone give no facts to write claims from.

## A debrief title set, pattern "Bottom line, then the causal chain"

For contrast, a debrief of a billing outage for the wider company, read alone:

1. Billing was down for 3 hours 10 minutes on the 4th; no customer was charged twice, and service has been normal since.
2. A routine certificate renewal failed, and because the alert went to a retired channel, nobody saw it for two hours.
3. Three conditions made that possible: one person held the renewal knowledge, the alert route was never tested, and the runbook pointed to the old channel.
4. Two fixes are done and one is due on the 20th, each with an owner.
5. We do not yet know why the renewal failed in the first place; the vendor's analysis is due Friday.

Five pages and no ask. A reader who stops after the first has the outcome, and each later title is one link in the chain.
