# The evidence behind the rules

This is the short version of the research the skill is built on, for when the author asks "why?" or a rule seems not to fit. Strength is marked: **strong** (meta-analyses, replicated experiments, large field experiments), **experimental** (one or two controlled studies), **consensus** (practitioner canon and institutional doctrine agree, and nobody has tested it on audiences), **contested** (credible evidence on both sides) and **untested**.

Read three cautions before quoting any of it.

1. **The subjects were students.** Almost every experiment tested students learning unfamiliar material for a test minutes later, or consumers, patients and mock jurors reading a short message. Section 7 lists the few studies of professionals. Transfer to executives deciding in a meeting is assumed throughout.
2. **The effects are small.** A review of 30 meta-analyses of message design found small average differences and prediction intervals that usually include zero ([O'Keefe & Hoeken 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.664160)). Present every rule as a tilt.
3. **Deck structure is mostly canon.** No experiment found compares two orders of a whole deck on any professional audience. The sequence rules are practice that agrees with itself.

Effect sizes d and g are standardized mean differences: 0.2 is conventionally small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large.

## 1. Slides as such

- **Having slides does not change how much people learn (strong).** 48 studies comparing the same material with and without slides: g = 0.067, 95% CI -0.103 to 0.236. School pupils gained and college students did not. [Baker et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2018.08.003).
- **In a simulated board pitch, a slide deck was rated no better than speaking with no visuals (experimental).** Two experiments with live delivery and random assignment. Memory and comprehension did not differ by format. [Moulton, Türkay & Kosslyn 2017](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0178774).
- **Real decks break basic perception and memory rules almost every time, in business as in academia (experimental, descriptive).** 140 decks sampled from the web, every one flawed. The most broken: text that cannot be seen, too much on the slide, changes in appearance that mean nothing. Audiences reported being annoyed and often could not say what was wrong. The paper did not test whether the flaws harm comprehension. [Kosslyn et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00230).
- **Slides are also working objects that teams negotiate around (field studies).** [Kaplan 2011](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.1100.0531); [Knight, Paroutis & Heracleous 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/smj.2727). Comprehension tests do not measure this use.

## 2. The medium: who sets the pace

- **Design matters most when the audience cannot control the pace and the material is complex (strong).** An overview of 29 reviews, 1,189 studies. [Noetel et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329).
- **Pictures with spoken words beat pictures with printed words when the presentation is system-paced, and the advantage shrinks or reverses when readers set the pace (strong on the boundary, contested in size).** [Ginns 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2005.07.001) found the pacing moderator. A self-paced classroom study found the reverse effect ([Tabbers et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1348/000709904322848824)). A later re-analysis reports the average effect falling well below the first estimate after correcting for publication bias ([Reinwein 2012](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10936-011-9180-4); the corrected figure was not confirmed at the source for this file). Long spoken passages also reverse it ([Leahy & Sweller 2011](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1787)).
- **A self-paced reader understands as well as a listener, and better on inference (strong).** 46 studies, N = 4,687: no overall difference (g = 0.07); reading ahead when self-paced (g = 0.13) and for inferential comprehension (g = 0.36). [Clinton-Lisell 2021](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211060871). Reading on screens costs a little against paper, more under time pressure ([Delgado et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.003)).
- **Text-heavy slides pull attention from what the speaker says (experimental).** [Savoy et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2008.12.005); [Wecker 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2012.01.013).
- **Repeating the narration on screen is mixed, and turns on how much text there is (contested).** The full script beside a graphic hurts. A few key terms help, most when the audience cannot rewind. [Adesope & Nesbit 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026147); [Mayer & Johnson 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.100.2.380); [Yue, Bjork & Bjork 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031971).
- **Spoken information vanishes, so what must be held or compared should stay visible (experimental).** [Singh, Marcus & Ayres 2012](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.2885).
- **On screens most people scan, and headings turn the scan into a useful one (consensus from usability studies, with eye-tracking support for headings).** [Nielsen 2008](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/); [Pernice 2017](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/); [Hyönä & Lorch 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2004.01.001). The data are web pages, and no eye-tracking exists for a deck read alone.
- **Adults read non-fiction at about 240 words a minute (strong).** [Brysbaert 2019](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/xynwg).
- **Slides, slide documents and small-room decks are separate artifacts (consensus).** Duarte, [Slidedocs](https://www.duarte.com/slidedocs/); Reynolds, *Presentation Zen*; Abela, *Advanced Presentations by Design*; [Fowler on the "slideument"](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/Slideument.html). Whether one deck can serve both is contested and untested.
- **Handouts given before a talk were neutral or helpful in the lab, and mixed at course level (contested).** [Marsh & Sink 2010](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1579).
- **What a reading deck must print (consensus, synthesized).** The eight-item list in `medium.md` is assembled from the sources above and from accounts of narrative-memo practice. It has not been tested as a checklist.

## 3. Explaining complex material

- **The multimedia learning principles hold in direction, at about half the size of the famous lab figures (strong).** Independent meta-analyses: marking what matters, d about 0.38 ([Alpizar et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11423-020-09748-7)); words next to the thing they describe, g = 0.63 ([Schroeder & Cenkci 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9435-9)); presenting in segments, small to medium ([Rey et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4)). The larger medians come from one lab's short, system-paced lessons ([Mayer 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4614-3185-5_31)).
- **Interesting but irrelevant material lowers learning (strong).** [Sundararajan & Adesope 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4). Untested for buy-in.
- **Naming the parts before explaining the mechanism helps newcomers (strong in lab studies, mostly one group).** Mayer 2014, above.
- **Help for newcomers can slow experts (strong for the interaction, untested in decks).** [Kalyuga 2007](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-007-9054-3).
- **A preview helps a little, more for unfamiliar material (strong that it exists and is small).** [Luiten, Ames & Ackerson 1980](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312017002211), 135 studies. Spoken signposts greatly increased what listeners recorded ([Titsworth & Kiewra 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2003.12.001)). The agenda slide as used in business decks is untested.
- **A frame given before a passage improves comprehension and recall, and the same frame given afterwards does little (strong, classic).** [Bransford & Johnson 1972](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(72)80006-9).
- **Overviews are liked more reliably than they help (experimental, mixed).** "Overview first, zoom and filter, then details on demand" was offered as a starting point with no experiment behind it ([Shneiderman 1996](https://doi.org/10.1109/VL.1996.545307)). A controlled study found users preferred an overview and were no more accurate with it ([Hornbæk, Bederson & Plaisant 2002](https://doi.org/10.1145/586081.586086)).
- **Animation helps a little when the content is itself a process (strong, small).** [Berney & Bétrancourt 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2016.06.005).
- **Step-by-step reveals are contested.** Segmenting supports them for complex explanations. One classroom study found animated builds did worse than showing the slide whole ([Mahar et al. 2009](https://www.isedj.org/7/82/ISEDJ.7(82).Mahar.pdf)).
- **Working memory holds about four chunks (strong), which supports "few groups" and no particular bullet count (untested as a slide rule).** [Cowan 2001](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922).
- **Curiosity about a question improves memory for its answer (experimental, replicated).** [Loewenstein 1994](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75); [Kang et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02402.x).
- **Asking an audience to retrieve keeps them on task (strong for the testing effect, experimental during a talk).** [Szpunar, Khan & Schacter 2013](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1221764110).
- **No attention cliff at 10 to 15 minutes (strong that the number is unsupported).** Attention drifts with time and recovers on a change of activity. [Wilson & Korn 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/00986280701291291); [Bradbury 2016](https://doi.org/10.1152/advan.00109.2016).

## 4. The slide

- **A sentence headline over visual evidence beat a topic title over bullets (experimental).** 110 engineering undergraduates, one recorded talk: comprehension d = 0.81, fewer misconceptions, better recall at ten days. The study is by the method's authors. [Garner & Alley 2013](https://www.ijee.ie/articles/Vol29-6/23_ijee2791ns.pdf). Medical-school cohort studies agree, without random assignment ([Issa et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1111/medu.12127)). No study changes the headline alone.
- **Less text per slide went with better ratings, and slide count did not matter (experimental, correlational).** Brock, Joglekar & Cohen 2011, as reported in a later review. This is the only empirical touchpoint for word limits.
- **Managers received a strategy more favourably as a diagram than as the same words in bullets, with no gain in understanding (experimental, one study).** 76 managers. [Kernbach, Eppler & Bresciani 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/2329488414525444). The no-gain-in-understanding result and the better ratings were confirmed from abstracts; finer outcomes were not.
- **A caveat buried under a reassuring title can hide the fact the decision turns on (consensus, case study).** The Columbia Accident Investigation Board used one briefing slide as an illustration of poor technical communication, among organizational causes. [Tufte's analysis](https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/columbia-accident-investigation-board-the-boeing-powerpoint-slide/); [the Board's report](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/columbia/reports/CAIBreportv1.pdf). The Board did not find that a slide caused the loss.

## 5. Structure and sequence

- **Answer first: one governing thought, then the grouped reasons (consensus).** Minto, *The Pyramid Principle*; military "bottom line up front"; intelligence key judgments ([ICD 203](https://www.dni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-203.pdf)). Indirect support: explicit conclusions (section 6), the frame-first result (section 3), and first-position advantage for engaged audiences ([Haugtvedt & Wegener 1994](https://doi.org/10.1086/209393), students). The head-to-head test on decision makers has not been run.
- **Claim titles, the titles test and outlining before building (consensus).** Consulting practice; Zelazny, *Say It With Presentations*; Atkinson, *Beyond Bullet Points*; Reynolds; Duarte. Every school prescribes planning the claims before opening the software, which makes it the most widely shared rule in the canon. Untested.
- **The four-sentence opening and "and, but, therefore" (consensus).** Minto; Olson, *Houston, We Have a Narrative*.
- **Briefings typed by purpose, with the decision brief stating the recommendation before the facts (consensus, doctrine).** US Army FM 6-0, chapter 7 ([2014 edition](https://archive.org/details/fm-6-0-commander-and-staff-organization-and-operations-2014)).
- **Facts kept apart from judgments, likelihood kept apart from confidence (consensus, doctrine).** ICD 203, above. The directive does not define confidence levels.
- **Probability words are read inconsistently, and a number beside the word fixes most of it (strong).** [Budescu et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/nclimate2194); [Mandel & Irwin 2021](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1930297500008603), N = 1,202.
- **Situation first, owned actions last, in handoffs and incident reviews (strong for one clinical bundle in its home setting, consensus elsewhere).** The I-PASS handoff bundle cut medical errors by 23% across nine programs ([Starmer et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMsa1405556)). The evidence for the simpler SBAR format is moderate ([Müller et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2018-022202)). Incident review practice: [Site Reliability Engineering, ch. 15](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/). None of these has been tested as a deck structure.
- **The contrast between what is and what could be (consensus, weak).** Duarte, *Resonate*. Derived from celebrated speeches with no comparison set. The same author advises [summary first for executives](https://www.duarte.com/blog/how-to-effectively-present-to-senior-executives/).
- **Narrative order against summary-first order for comprehension (contested).** News-reading experiments lean toward a linear build for suspense and for newcomers' understanding ([Knobloch et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1177/0093650203261517)). The business canon leans toward the summary for a reader who may stop early. They measure different outcomes.
- **Plot structures as deck templates (untested).** The hero's journey, five-act arcs and story spines have no audience test as deck structures.
- **Slides against prose for serious reasoning (contested).** Narrative-memo practice and Tufte hold that bullets drop the reasoning. The replies hold that the fault is the bulleted outline, and the format can carry sentences ([Farkas 2006](https://doi.org/10.1075/idj.14.2.08far); [Norman](https://jnd.org/in-defense-of-powerpoint/)). Both sides condemn topic titles over nested fragments. No comparison of memo against deck has been published.

## 6. Winning a decision

- **Stating the conclusion and a specific recommendation persuades more than leaving it implied, with no reversal for more capable audiences (strong, small).** O'Keefe 1997 and 2002, summarized in [O'Keefe & Hoeken 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.664160).
- **Answering the opposing argument beats ignoring it, and raising it unanswered is worst (strong, small).** [O'Keefe 1999](https://doi.org/10.1080/23808985.1999.11678963); [Allen 1991](https://doi.org/10.1080/10570319109374395).
- **Weak arguments subtract from strong ones (experimental, replicated).** [Weaver, Garcia & Schwarz 2012](https://doi.org/10.1086/664497); [Obermaier & Koch 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-73348-1). The same averaging makes a serious risk look smaller among trivial ones ([Sivanathan & Kakkar 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-017-0223-1)).
- **Stories and statistics roughly tie on persuasion (strong that there is no blanket winner).** [Xu 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/10410236.2022.2137750); [Freling et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2020.01.006); [Zebregs et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1080/10410236.2013.842528). Mostly health messages.
- **A story's effect on belief outlasts a statistic's (experimental).** The effect of a statistic faded by 73% over a day and a story's by 32%. [Graeber, Roth & Zimmermann 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae020).
- **Absorption in a story lowers counter-arguing (strong).** [Green & Brock 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.701); [van Laer et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1086/673383). This is the basis for the honest-persuasion checks.
- **An audience with a stake judges argument quality, and one without goes by the source and the look (strong as a framework, from older student studies).** [Petty, Cacioppo & Goldman 1981](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.41.5.847). People who must justify a decision think harder about it ([Lerner & Tetlock 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.2.255)).
- **The default option is chosen far more often (strong).** 58 studies, d = 0.68. [Jachimowicz et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1017/bpp.2018.43).
- **A single option shown alone sends people looking for alternatives (experimental).** [Mochon 2013](https://doi.org/10.1086/671343). More options do not reliably paralyse ([Scheibehenne et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1086/651235)).
- **Volunteering a weakness raises credibility (strong for two-sided messages).** [Eisend 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2005.11.001); [Williams et al. 1993](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01044684).
- **A numeric range costs little trust, and vague verbal hedging costs more (strong).** Five experiments, one in the field. [van der Bles et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1913678117).
- **People underestimate their own timelines (strong for the bias).** [Buehler, Griffin & Ross 1994](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366). Estimating from comparable past work is the usual remedy (consensus).
- **A risk lands better with its remedy attached (strong, public health messages).** [Tannenbaum et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0039729).
- **Credibility set before the argument shapes how it is read (strong, older studies).** [O'Keefe 1987](https://doi.org/10.1080/10510978709368231).
- **Groups discuss what everyone knows and neglect what one person knows (strong).** [Stasser & Titus 1985](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.48.6.1467); [Lu, Yuan & McLeod 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868311417243).
- **Selling an issue upward: tie it to the leaders' goals, involve others early, time it, bring a solution (field and survey work).** [Dutton et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.5465/3069412). Building agreement in conversations before the meeting is near-universal advice with no controlled test (consensus).
- **Anticipating objections (consensus).** Kotter and Whitehead, *Buy-In*.
- **Gain against loss framing makes almost no difference on average (strong).** [Gallagher & Updegraff 2012](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12160-011-9308-7).
- **The line between persuasion and manipulation (consensus, philosophy).** Persuasion engages the audience's ability to deliberate, and a workable test is whether the technique would survive being disclosed. [Sunstein 2016](https://doi.org/10.1561/107.00000014).

## 7. Studies of professionals

These are the only findings here that studied managers, investors or producers deciding about real resources.

- Managers and a strategy shown as bullets or diagrams: Kernbach et al. 2015 (section 4).
- Managers' own accounts of selling issues upward: Dutton et al. 2001 (section 6).
- Investors funded pitchers who looked prepared ([Chen, Yao & Kotha 2009](https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2009.36462018)); gestures that depicted the idea raised investor interest ([Clarke, Cornelissen & Healey 2019](https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2016.1008)); early-stage investors decide largely on their read of the person ([Huang & Pearce 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0001839215597270)).
- People hearing a pitch judge the pitcher as much as the idea, and rate pitchers higher when drawn into shaping it ([Elsbach & Kramer 2003](https://doi.org/10.2307/30040623), one industry).
- The simulated board pitch: Moulton et al. 2017 (section 1), recruited adults in role.

## Folklore to avoid

| Claim | What the record says |
|---|---|
| "Visuals are processed 60,000 times faster than text" | No study exists. It traces to promotional material with no named source. |
| "People remember 10% of what they read, 20% of what they hear..." | The percentages were added by others to a model that carried no numbers. |
| "Attention spans are eight seconds" and "attention drops after 10 minutes" | The first traces to a marketing report with no source. Reviews of the second found no threshold in the primary data. |
| "7% words, 38% tone, 55% body language" | The experiments were about judging liking when words and tone conflict. They say nothing about a briefing's content. |
| "Seven plus or minus two bullets" | A rough figure for recall of unrelated items. The current estimate is about four, and neither was a study of slides. |
| The 6x6 and 7x7 rules, 10/20/30, slides per minute | Personal rules of thumb. No study was found for any of them. |
| "Stories are 22 times more memorable than facts" | No source in the work of either person it is attributed to. The real results are a 1969 word-list mnemonic study and Graeber et al. 2024. |
| "63% remember the story, 5% the statistic" | A classroom exercise described in a trade book. An illustration at most. |
| "One identifiable victim beats statistics" | Pooled effect r = .05 ([Lee & Feeley 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/15534510.2016.1216891)), and a preregistered replication found none ([Maier et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.90203)). |
| "Charts make a claim more believable" | Four replications found no added persuasiveness ([Dragicevic & Jansen 2018](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2017.2744298)). |
| "Losses loom twice as large, so frame as a loss" | Loss aversion as a general law is disputed ([Gal & Rucker 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcpy.1047)), and loss framing shows no persuasive edge. |
| "Too many options paralyse" | The meta-analytic mean is about zero. |
| "Experts persuade more when they sound unsure" | A registered replication with 1,018 participants found plain confidence helped ([Løhre et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/gjxyh)). |
| "Spoilers improve a story, so give away the ending" or the reverse | Mixed results, all on fiction. No support either way for decks. |
| "A decoy option steers the choice" | Fragile outside stylized tables ([Frederick, Lee & Baskin 2014](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.12.0061)). |
| "Smart audiences prefer to draw their own conclusions" | Explicit conclusions persuaded more with no reversal for capable audiences. |
| "A slide caused the Columbia accident" | The Board cited a slide as an illustration among organizational causes. |
| "One company proved memos beat slides" | Its leaders gave reasons. No comparison has been published. |
| "The consulting firms proved answer first works" | It is a house method that spread. No audience experiment was found. |
| "People read web pages in an F, so lay content out in an F" | The pattern is what readers fall back on when a page lacks headings. The advice is to prevent it. |

## Known gaps

- Answer first against a build, on decision makers, for comprehension, agreement or decision quality.
- Any whole-deck order or story arc against another.
- The same content as a read-alone deck and as a presented deck, or a slide document against a prose memo.
- How many people read an internal pre-read, for how long, and whether they open notes or appendix pages.
- Claim titles alone, with the slide body held constant.
- One idea per slide, word limits, slide counts.
- The agenda slide and the closing summary slide.
- Whether a meeting that starts from questions beats one that re-presents after a pre-read.
- Whether stories help or hurt with accountable, scrutinizing decision makers.
- How much scaffolding to remove for an expert room.
- Whether any briefing format from a high-reliability field improves outcomes when carried into business decks.
- Concreteness and psychological distance as applied to an ask: not researched for this file.

Figures in this file were taken from abstracts or full texts where the note says so, and otherwise from secondary reports. Check a figure against its source before quoting it to a board, a regulator or the public.
