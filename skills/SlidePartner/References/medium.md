# The medium: live, read alone, or both

Decide the medium before writing a word on any slide. The storyline (the ordered claims) is the same in every medium. The rendering is different, and the differences run in opposite directions.

## Three kinds of deck

| Kind | Setting | Who carries the explanation | What the slide is |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Live, large room** | A speaker, a screen, an audience that listens | The speaker | A visual aid: one picture and a few words |
| **Live, small room** | A handful of people around a table, discussing | The speaker and the page together | A page people look at and point to, denser than a keynote slide |
| **Read alone** | A pre-read, a leave-behind, a deck forwarded with no speaker | The page | A page of a short illustrated document |

Practitioners name these differently (ballroom and conference-room style, presentation and slide document). They agree that the kinds are separate artifacts and that a file trying to be all of them fails at each (consensus).

## Why the word rules are opposite

When the presenter sets the pace, pictures with spoken words teach better than pictures with printed words. The effect is real and smaller than first reported, and it depends on pacing: it is strongest when the audience cannot stop or rewind, and it shrinks or reverses when readers control the pace (strong on the boundary, contested in size). Separately, a self-paced reader understands as well as a listener and does better on inference (strong). Text-heavy slides pull attention from what the speaker says (experimental).

Read together: in a room, words on the slide compete with the voice. Alone, words on the page are the only voice there is. All of this was measured on students learning, and the transfer to professionals deciding is assumed.

## Rendering rules

| Element | Presented live | Read alone |
|---|---|---|
| **Title** | The claim as a sentence, two lines at most | The claim as a sentence |
| **Body text** | A few key terms that the speaker also says. Never the script, least of all beside a figure | Full sentences that carry the reasoning: because, therefore, despite |
| **Where words sit** | On the figure, at the point they describe | The same, with the explanation next to the part it explains |
| **How to read a chart** | The speaker says it | Printed on the page: what the axes are, what to look at, what to conclude |
| **Terms** | Defined aloud at first use, and shown if the argument runs on them | Defined on the page at first use, with a glossary in the appendix for a mixed audience |
| **What must be held in mind** | Kept on the slide: numbers to compare, the decision asked for, the steps of a process | On the page already. The reader can go back |
| **Complex diagram** | Built across several slides, one layer each, with the finished diagram left up long enough to take in | A short run of pages, one layer each |
| **Position in the deck** | Spoken signposts, and a map that returns at each section | In a deck longer than about ten pages, a tracker on every page and section dividers. A tracker is a small strip naming the deck's sections with the current one marked |
| **Detail and backup** | Appendix, pulled up when asked | Appendix, linked from the page it supports |
| **Sources** | Small, present, and said aloud when they matter | Footnoted on the page |
| **Opening** | The frame and the ask in the first minute | A summary page a reader can stop after |
| **Closing** | The ask and what happens next | The ask, what happens next, and who to contact |

Density is a matter of grouping. Working memory holds about four chunks, which supports "few groups per slide" and supports no particular count of bullets or words (strong for the capacity estimate, untested as a slide rule). Limit what is on each slide and leave the number of slides free.

## What a speaker supplies, and a page must print

Before a deck goes out to be read alone, walk it as a reader who cannot reach the author and check eight things. The list is a synthesis of practitioner sources and has not been tested as a checklist.

1. The answer on the first page, and the ask when the deck has one.
2. The reasoning between slides: why this follows from that. Sentence titles and a visible storyline carry it.
3. Definitions of terms and acronyms at first use.
4. How to read each chart, on the chart's page.
5. Where the reader is. A deck of about ten pages or fewer needs only its summary page. A longer one needs sections and a tracker, and a complex subject needs a map.
6. Sources and assumptions.
7. Answers to the predictable questions, in an appendix or a short question-and-answer section.
8. What happens next, and who decides.

## One storyline, two renderings

Experts disagree on whether one deck can serve both. One camp says never (slides, notes and a separate handout). Consulting practice makes the deck the document on purpose. The experiments on pacing favour two renderings. No study compares a hybrid against two purpose-built decks (contested).

The workable practice:

- **Pick the primary medium, build it, derive the other.** The titles stay the same in both.
- **With time for only one, build the read-alone version.** Present from a stripped copy with the detail pages hidden or moved to the appendix. This ordering is an inference from the pacing evidence and is flagged as such.
- **Do not rely on speaker notes to reach a reader.** Recipients usually see slide view, where notes are hidden, and nobody has measured how often they open them. If notes must carry the explanation, export notes pages so each printed page pairs the slide with its text.
- **Send the reading version after a talk.** The live slides alone will not make sense to someone who was not there.

## The pre-read and the meeting after it

- **Do not assume it was read.** No published figure exists for how many people read an internal pre-read or how long they spend. Some organizations read in silence at the start of the meeting for this reason. Either allow reading time or open with the one-page summary.
- **Size it.** Adults read non-fiction at roughly 240 words a minute before any thinking time. Count the words and say how long the read takes. While the deck is still a storyline, set a word budget per page and state the reading time that budget implies.
- **Write for scanning.** On screens most people scan, and headings turn the scan into a useful one. The claim titles and the summary page are what a scanning reader gets, so they must carry the argument alone.
- **Change the meeting.** When people have read, do not re-present. Bring the short discussion deck from `storylines.md`: a one-page recap that also serves anyone who has not read, the open questions, the decision.
- **Sending slides ahead does not hurt the live session.** The direct tests found handouts given beforehand were neutral or helpful (contested at course level).

## Senior audiences

Published advice on briefing executives is consistent and comes from experience, with no studies behind it (consensus). State the summary and the ask at the start. Expect to be interrupted and let the group drive. Expect most of the deck to live in the appendix and be pulled up on demand. Build the deck so it survives being cut to a third of its time: the first slide alone, then the first three, should each stand as a complete message.

The more uncertain the proposal, the more the decision rests on trust in the people who will run it. A deck for a speculative bet should spend real space on who will do the work and why they can.

## Expert and mixed audiences

Help that works for newcomers can slow experts. For an expert room, cut the definitions and the step-by-step build and go to the claim and the evidence. For a mixed room, put the scaffolding in the pre-read or the appendix and run the live session at the experts' level. No study says how much to remove.

## When a deck is the wrong artifact

- **A long causal argument for a decision.** Sentences and paragraphs hold reasoning that bullet fragments drop. Offer a memo. If a deck is required, keep full-sentence titles and write the relations between ideas on the page.
- **Reference material.** A table or a document people look things up in is not read in order and gains nothing from a storyline.
- **A talk that needs no visuals.** A well-argued talk with no slides is a legitimate choice. Slides were rated no better than speaking alone in the one live test.

Critics of slides and their defenders condemn the same thing: topic titles over nested bullet fragments, in a file that tries to be both slides and document. Attack that failure and leave the format alone.
