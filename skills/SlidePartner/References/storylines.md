# Storyline patterns

A storyline is the deck written as an ordered list of claims. Each pattern below gives a sequence, the reason for its order and how well that order is supported. The patterns are starting shapes. Rename the steps into real claim titles for the content at hand, merge steps a short deck does not need, and split any step that carries more than one claim.

Grades follow `evidence.md`: **strong**, **experimental**, **consensus** (practitioners agree, nobody has tested it on audiences), **contested**, **untested**. Most sequence advice is consensus. No experiment has compared two whole-deck orders on a professional audience.

## What "a journey" means here

Every method in the canon shares two things: a tension the audience recognizes at the start, and a sequence where each step follows from the last. Underneath their vocabulary, the story methods and the logic methods are the same shape: an opening that raises a question, then a hierarchy of claims that answers it.

So the working definition is a journey with the destination announced:

1. **Frame first.** Say what the deck is about and what it asks of the audience. Giving the frame before the material improves comprehension and recall, and giving it afterwards does little (strong, from reading research).
2. **A question at each step.** Open each section with the question the audience already half holds, then answer it promptly. People remember answers to questions they were curious about (experimental, replicated).
3. **Each step earns the next.** The title of one slide should make the audience want the next. Read the titles as a paragraph to check.
4. **A map that returns.** For complex material, show the whole early and bring it back with the current part marked.
5. **Land where you said you would.** The last slide answers the first.

Hiding the destination is the exception. It is advised only for a live, persuasive talk to an audience that must first be brought to care, and that advice comes from close reading of famous speeches with no comparison set (consensus, weak). For a solitary reader, no method prescribes it and several forbid it.

## Openings that work for any pattern

Write four sentences before choosing a pattern: the **situation** the audience already accepts, the **complication** that disturbs it, the **question** that raises, and the **answer**. In an answer-first deck all four go on the first content slide or the summary page. In a signalled build, the first three open the deck and the answer comes after the options.

A shorter test is one sentence in the form "and, but, therefore". A summary with no "but" describes a situation and argues nothing.

## Debrief patterns

A debrief aims at understanding. It has no call to action, though it often ends by naming the choices the findings open. Do not give a debrief a persuasive arc. If the author needs a decision, the deck is a buy-in deck with a debrief inside it.

### Findings first

**Use when** the deck reports an assessment, an analysis or research results, and some of it is uncertain.

| Step | What the slide claims |
|---|---|
| 1 | The question that was asked, why it matters now, and the answer in one sentence |
| 2 | The three or four findings, each as a sentence, each marked with how sure the authors are |
| 3 | The map: how the findings relate and how the deck is laid out |
| 4 to n | One finding per section: the claim, the evidence, what is measured and what is judged, and what would change the conclusion |
| n+1 | What is not known, and what would settle it |
| n+2 | What this opens: the decisions or next questions, with owners if they exist |
| Appendix | Method, data, definitions, the analyses that did not make the main line |

**Why this order.** A reader who stops after the second slide still has the findings. Intelligence analysis standards require the main judgments up front, facts kept apart from judgments, and likelihood kept apart from confidence. Those standards are doctrine (consensus), built for readers who act on incomplete information.

**Uncertainty.** When the authors have a number, print it beside the probability word: "likely (about 70%)". When they have none, ask for their estimate and its basis, and never supply one. Readers assign widely different probabilities to the same word, and the number alone communicates as well as the word (experimental, one large study). Give ranges with what they rest on. A numeric range costs little trust, and vague hedging costs more (strong).

**Method.** State the finding, then give only enough method for the audience to trust it. The order in which the work was done is the wrong order for the audience (consensus, scientific-talk canon).

### Whole, parts, interactions

**Use when** the audience has to understand how a system, a process or a subject works, and most of them are new to it.

| Step | What the slide claims |
|---|---|
| 1 | The question this explanation answers for this audience, and the one-sentence model |
| 2 | The whole: one diagram of the system at a single level of detail |
| 3 | The parts, named in the audience's own words |
| 4 to n | One part or one layer per slide, added to the same diagram, with the new element marked and the title stating what it does |
| n+1 | The interactions: the two or three slides where several parts must be understood together |
| n+2 | Where it breaks, what it does not cover, what is still being worked out |
| n+3 | What this means for the audience's work |

**Why this order.** Naming the parts before explaining the mechanism improves understanding for newcomers (strong within lab studies, mostly one research group). Presenting a complex explanation in segments improves retention and transfer by a small to medium amount (strong). An overview given before the material helps a little, more for unfamiliar subjects (strong that the effect exists and is small).

**Spend the effort where the parts interact.** Design matters most when many elements must be held at once. A list of independent facts needs far less care (strong in education research).

**The map costs attention if the audience has to flip back.** Keep the same shapes and labels on every slide, with a small marked copy of the whole. People like overviews more reliably than they benefit from them (experimental, mixed).

**Experts.** Cut steps 3 and most of 4 for an expert room. Scaffolding that helps newcomers slows people who know the domain (strong for the interaction, untested in decks).

### Bottom line, then the causal chain

**Use when** the deck explains what happened: an incident, an outage, a result that surprised people, a change in a market.

| Step | What the slide claims |
|---|---|
| 1 | What happened, the impact, and the state of things now |
| 2 | The chain: each event linked to the next by its cause, on one timeline |
| 3 to n | The conditions that made the chain possible, with what is established and what is judged |
| n+1 | What has changed already, and what will, with owners and dates |
| n+2 | What remains open or unknown |
| Appendix | The detailed timeline, the data, the alternatives ruled out |

**Why this order.** Post-incident and clinical handoff formats put the situation and its severity first and end with owned actions. The handoff bundles have outcome evidence in their home setting (strong there, untested for decks).

**Cause over chronology.** Link the steps with "because of that" so the audience gets a chain and not a list of timestamps. A chronological order inside this section is right: a newcomer follows a causal chain best in the order it happened (contested, small experiments on news readers).

**Blame.** Describe conditions and decisions as the system produced them. A slide that names a person as the cause ends the audience's learning.

### Headline, changes, exceptions

**Use when** the deck is a recurring readout: status, results, a risk report.

| Step | What the slide claims |
|---|---|
| 1 | The headline against the plan or the last report, in one sentence |
| 2 | What changed since last time and why |
| 3 to n | The exceptions that need attention, worst first, each with its cause and its owner |
| n+1 | What is needed from this audience, if anything |
| n+2 | What to expect next period |
| Appendix | The full tables |

**Why this order.** The audience has seen the frame before, so the new information is the change and the exceptions. Keep the layout identical from one period to the next so that a difference on the page is a difference in the facts. If step n+1 is a real decision, give it its own slide with the ask written as an action.

## Buy-in patterns

A buy-in deck exists to get a decision. State the ask in plain words in every pattern. Stating the conclusion and a specific recommendation persuades more than leaving the audience to infer it, with no reversal for more capable audiences (strong, small effect).

### Ask first

**Use when** the decision maker is receptive or neutral, short of time, and expects a recommendation. This is the default.

| Step | What the slide claims |
|---|---|
| 1 | The ask: who does what, by when, at what cost, and why now |
| 2 | The problem, made concrete with one case and the base rate that case stands for |
| 3 | The recommendation and what it changes |
| 4 to 6 | The strongest reasons, one per slide, at most three |
| 7 | The options considered on the same few criteria, including doing nothing and what that costs |
| 8 | The strongest objection, answered |
| 9 | The main weakness and risk: its size, its likelihood, the remedy, and what would reverse the recommendation |
| 10 | Cost, timeline as a range from comparable work, and who will run it |
| 11 | The ask again, and the next step if the answer is yes |
| Appendix | Remaining objections with answers, detailed numbers, method |

**Why this order.** Consulting, military and intelligence practice all put the conclusion first for a senior audience (consensus). The indirect experimental support: explicit conclusions persuade more, an engaged audience weighs what comes first more heavily (experimental, students), and a frame given first aids comprehension. The direct test of answer first against a build, on decision makers, has not been run.

**Step 2.** A story and a statistic roughly tie on persuasion, and the best-supported benefit of the story is memory. In one set of experiments a statistic's effect on belief faded by about 73% in a day and a story's by about 32% (experimental). Pair the case with the number. One vivid case does not outweigh the aggregate: that effect is near zero in replication (contested).

**Steps 4 to 6.** Three strong reasons beat three strong and two weak, because audiences average what they are given (experimental, replicated, on students and consumers). Three is a habit of the canon and no ceiling: the test is whether each reason is one the author would stake the case on.

**Step 7.** Say what happens with no decision, with its cost and date, so the status quo is one option among the others. The default option is chosen far more often (strong). A single option shown alone sends people looking for alternatives (experimental). Show two or three real options. A deliberately weak option is unreliable and reads as a trick.

**Steps 8 and 9.** Answering the opposing argument beats ignoring it, and raising it without answering is the worst of the three (strong, two meta-analyses). Volunteering a weakness raises credibility (strong). Concede a secondary point with its remedy attached, early enough that nobody else raises it first.

**Step 9, likelihood.** Where no likelihood can be sourced, ask the author for their own estimate and what it rests on, and label it as an estimate on the slide.

**Step 10.** Back the date with how long comparable work took. People underestimate their own timelines even when they know their record (strong for the bias). If nothing comparable exists, say so on the slide and give the range with its basis.

### Signalled build

**Use when** the room is opposed or sceptical and would reject the conclusion on sight.

| Step | What the slide claims |
|---|---|
| 1 | What is being decided today, and that the deck ends with a recommendation |
| 2 | The situation, stated so the audience agrees with it |
| 3 | The complication, with evidence from sources this audience trusts |
| 4 | The audience's objection, in its strongest form and in their words |
| 5 | The criteria a good answer has to meet |
| 6 | The options against those criteria, including the one the audience prefers |
| 7 | The recommendation |
| 8 | What the recommendation gives up, and how that is handled |
| 9 | The ask and the next step |

**Why this order.** The canon's own exception to answer first is the hostile reader (consensus). Agreeing criteria before options lets the audience judge the recommendation by a standard they accepted. Keep the delay short: the ask is delayed and never omitted. The position of the ask for a hostile audience has no experiment behind it (untested).

**If the presenter is junior or unknown to the room,** lead with the evidence and let the argument be judged first. If the presenter's standing is high, establish it and where the data came from at the start. Credibility set before the argument shapes how it is read (strong, older studies).

### Decision brief

**Use when** a formal body has to choose among defined options and expects to see them compared.

| Step | What the slide claims |
|---|---|
| 1 | The decision required, and the recommendation |
| 2 | The background the choice depends on: facts and stated assumptions |
| 3 | The options, each described fairly |
| 4 | The criteria and how each option does against them, in one table |
| 5 | The recommendation and the reasoning |
| 6 | What the decision sets in motion, and who needs to concur |

**Why this order.** This is the military decision briefing: decision and recommendation first, then facts, options, comparison (consensus, institutional doctrine). An older form walks the options before recommending. Use that only where the body's own custom requires it.

### The gap

**Use when** a speaker addresses a large, undecided group live and the goal is to move them toward a change. Never use it for a pre-read or a debrief.

| Step | What the talk does |
|---|---|
| 0 | In the first minute, the change this talk argues for, named in a sentence |
| 1 | What is: the present as the audience lives it |
| 2 | What could be: the first sight of the alternative |
| 3 | Back and forth between the two, each return adding one piece of evidence or one obstacle answered |
| 4 | The ask, stated plainly |
| 5 | What the audience gets if they act |

**Why this order.** The contrast between today and the proposed future, repeated, is the shape found in celebrated speeches (consensus, derived from successes only). The same author advises against it for executives, who should get the summary first. Treat the audience as the one who acts and the speaker as the guide. Naming the change at the start keeps the frame-first rule: the audience knows the subject and the direction, and the full ask waits for step 4.

**Leave room.** In live pitches, the people deciding rate pitchers higher when they are drawn into shaping the idea (field study, one industry). Leave one real design choice open and ask for their view.

### Discussion deck

**Use when** the audience has received a pre-read and the meeting is for questions and the decision.

| Step | What the slide claims |
|---|---|
| 1 | The pre-read in one page: the ask and the reasons, plus anything new since it went out |
| 2 | The questions the authors most want the room's view on (a question slide, with no claim title) |
| 3 | The decision asked for, with the options |
| Backup | The pre-read's pages, ready to pull up |

**Why this order.** Practitioner sources agree that a meeting after a pre-read should start from questions and the decision and should not re-present (consensus). Slide 1 is the recap, and it doubles as the way in for anyone who has not read. Groups talk about what everyone already knows and neglect what only one person knows (strong), so slide 1 also marks what is new and what only the authors know.

**Time.** Give most of the meeting to step 2. In a group, ask the most senior person to speak last so the room is not anchored.

## Choosing when the situation is mixed

- **A pre-read followed by a meeting.** Two decks. The pre-read takes whichever pattern above fits its purpose and audience, rendered to be read alone. The meeting takes the Discussion deck.
- **A debrief that needs a decision at the end.** Run the debrief pattern, then one decision slide with the ask as an action. If the decision is the real purpose, switch to Ask first and move the debrief material into the reasons and the appendix.
- **A senior audience and an opposed one.** The two rules collide here and nobody has tested it. Default to stating what is being decided and the recommendation in the first minute, then go straight to the objection and the criteria.
- **A mixed room of experts and newcomers.** Put definitions and the parts in a pre-read or an appendix and run the live deck at the experts' level, with the map as the shared anchor.
- **Five minutes where thirty were planned.** Any pattern should survive being cut to its first slide and to its first three. Build the short version first and expand it.

## Borrowed plot structures

The hero's journey, five-act arcs and story spines describe drama and fiction. Nothing tests them as deck structures, and the business methods that claim them turn out to be an opening tension over a hierarchy of claims. Borrow two things: a causal chain ("because of that") when explaining how something came about, and the audience as the one who acts. Leave the plot stages out.
