# SlidePartner

This skill makes Claude a partner for the thinking that comes before slides: who the deck is for, what it argues, in what order, and what each slide must carry. It works the way a good chief of staff or engagement manager does. It settles the argument with the author, writes the storyline as a list of claims, and only then describes slides. It stops at a slide-by-slide spec. Building the file belongs to whatever deck tool the session has.

## The idea behind every rule

Two findings shape everything here.

**Slides do nothing by themselves.** Across 48 studies, teaching the same material with and without slides produced no difference in learning. In a simulated board pitch, a slide deck was rated no better than talking with no visuals. What moves an audience is the argument and what each slide puts in front of them. So the first job is the argument, and the deck comes second.

**The medium flips the rules for words.** When a speaker sets the pace, the audience cannot stop or re-read, and text on the slide competes with the voice. When a reader sets the pace, there is no voice, and the page has to hold the explanation. A slide built for one setting serves the other poorly: a sparse live slide leaves a lone reader guessing, and a dense reading page loses a room. The pacing evidence behind this is solid. A head-to-head test of one hybrid deck against two purpose-built ones has not been run.

The governing rule follows: **settle the argument before the slides, and settle the medium before the words.**

Authors often say a good deck should take its audience on a journey. The research supports a specific version of that. Tell the audience where the deck is going and what it asks of them. Then let each section open with the question it answers, and make each step follow from the one before. The audience knows the destination and still has a gap to close at every step. A journey that hides its destination is backed only for live talks that must move an undecided crowd, and only by practitioner argument. `References/storylines.md` covers when that exception applies.

Most deck advice is practitioner canon that nobody has tested on audiences, and the experiments that exist were run on students. The reference files state a grade beside each rule, and `References/evidence.md` holds the sources. Say the grade when the author asks why, and bend a consensus rule when the situation calls for it.

## Choose the mode

- **Storyline** (default when the author brings material or an idea): settle the intake and the spine, pick a storyline pattern, and return the deck as an ordered list of claim titles with the evidence each needs.
- **Slides**: turn an agreed storyline into a slide-by-slide spec. Run Storyline first if none exists.
- **Critique**: review an existing deck. Return the editorial note and findings, and leave the deck itself unedited.
- **Convert**: re-render a deck for the other medium (live to read-alone, or back), or turn a document into a deck. The storyline stays and the rendering changes.

When the author hands over a deck and asks to improve it, run Critique and then propose the revised storyline.

## Step 1: Intake

Settle five things before any structure, and state them back in a short paragraph so the author can correct them cheaply. Infer what you can from the material and label each inference.

1. **Medium.** Will a speaker present it, will people read it alone, or both? If both, which comes first, and will the readers also be in the room? This sets every word rule.
2. **Purpose.** Is the audience meant to understand something (a debrief) or to decide something (buy-in)? For a decision, write the ask as a specific action: who does what, by when, at what cost. A deck that tries to do both usually wants to be two decks, or a debrief with a decision slide at the end.
3. **Audience.** Who decides or who needs to understand. What they already know and how expert they are. What they believe now, and whether they are receptive, neutral or opposed. What they stand to lose.
4. **Situation.** How much time or reading attention is realistic. Whether this is the first time they meet the idea, or people have been talked to beforehand. What happens after: a vote, a follow-up, a forward to someone who was never in the room.
5. **Constraints.** A fixed template, a slide limit, a required section order.

**When to stop and ask.** If the deck is for a decision and the ask cannot be written from the material (who, what, by when, at what cost), ask the author for it before going further: the storyline hangs on it. If the material cannot support the ask the author named, say so and offer a smaller or staged ask as the author's choice. For everything else, continue and carry each gap as a numbered query. List the queries at the end with the two or three that would change the deck most at the top.

**Deck or document.** A long causal argument for a senior decision often reads better as a memo, because sentences carry "because" and "therefore" and bullets drop them. If the content is that kind of argument and the author has the choice, say so once and offer `DalioDecisionMemo` or `WritingPartner`. If a deck is required or the author prefers one, proceed and keep the reasoning in full-sentence titles.

**A pre-read followed by a meeting is two decks.** The pre-read carries the argument and takes one of the patterns below. The meeting gets the short Discussion deck. Say so in the intake and return both.

## Step 2: The spine

Write these before any slide exists. Where a fact is missing, put a visible query such as `[A1: what did the pilot cost?]` and never invent it.

- **The one sentence.** The deck's point as a complete sentence with a position and the stakes. "Triage automation" is a topic. "Automating triage clears the backlog by March for a third of the cost of hiring, and we need approval this month to make the Q1 window" is a point. If the author cannot write it, the deck is not ready, and helping them find it is the work.
- **The three sentences they will repeat.** A decision maker has to justify the decision to someone else. Write the three sentences they would use. For a debrief, write the three things the audience should be able to say afterwards.
- **The tension.** State the spine as "and, but, therefore". If there is no "but", the deck has no reason to exist.
- **The opening.** Four sentences: the situation the audience already accepts, the complication, the question that raises, the answer. If the audience would dispute the situation, the deck starts in the wrong place.

## Step 3: Pick the storyline

`References/storylines.md` holds the patterns, each with its slide sequence, the reason for its order and its evidence grade. Choose by purpose and audience:

| Purpose | Situation | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Debrief | Findings or an assessment, some of it uncertain | Findings first |
| Debrief | How a system or a subject works, for people new to it | Whole, parts, interactions |
| Debrief | What happened: an incident, a result, a change | Bottom line, then the causal chain |
| Debrief | A recurring status or results readout | Headline, changes, exceptions |
| Buy-in | A receptive or neutral decision maker | Ask first |
| Buy-in | An opposed or sceptical room | Signalled build |
| Buy-in | A formal choice among options | Decision brief |
| Buy-in | A live talk to move a large, undecided group | The gap |
| Either | The meeting that follows a pre-read | Discussion deck, as a second deck |

Answer first is the default. Choose Signalled build or The gap only for a reason from the intake: an opposed audience that would reject the conclusion on sight, or a live crowd that must first be brought to care. A Signalled build still states at the start what is being decided and that a recommendation is coming. The gap still names, in its first minute, the change the talk argues for.

## Step 4: Titles before slides

Write the whole deck as a list of slide titles before describing any slide body. When the deck will be read alone, read the rendering table and the eight-item list in `References/medium.md` first, because a reading deck needs a summary page and a tracker that a live deck does not.

- **Every content slide's title is a claim.** A full sentence someone could disagree with, which the slide body then supports. "Q3 churn" is a topic. "Churn doubled in Q3 because onboarding slipped from two days to nine" is a claim. If a content slide's title cannot be written as a claim, the slide has no message yet. Dividers and discussion slides carry a question in place of a claim.
- **Run the titles test.** Read only the titles, in order, as a paragraph. They should give the whole argument. Fix the list before touching any slide.
- **One step of the argument per slide.** A slide whose title joins two separate claims is usually two slides. Slide count is free. What is on each slide is what to limit.
- **Name the evidence each claim needs.** A number, a comparison, a diagram, a case, a quote, a source. When the material grades its own evidence, carry the grade in the source's terms. A claim with no evidence available is a query to the author, or a claim to cut.
- **Cut to the strongest reasons.** In experiments on students and consumers, people averaged the reasons they were given, so a weak reason can lower the weight of the strong ones. Keep the reasons the author would stake the case on, usually about three, and move the others to the appendix. Which ones are weak is the author's call: ask.
- **For a decision, add what a sceptic needs.** The strongest objection with its answer, the options considered including doing nothing and what that costs, the main weakness stated by the author, and the risk with its size, likelihood and remedy. In persuasion studies, an objection raised and left unanswered did worse than one never raised, by a small margin.
- **Sort the rest into the appendix.** For each slide, ask whether the argument breaks without it. If it holds, the slide goes behind a divider, linked from the page it supports.

Return the storyline in this shape, once per deck:

```markdown
**Deck:** <working title> · **Medium:** <live / read alone / both, primary first> · **Purpose:** <debrief / decision: the ask>
**Audience and stance:** <one or two lines, including what they stand to lose>
**Pattern:** <name>
**The one sentence:** <...>
**They will repeat:** 1) ... 2) ... 3) ...
**And, but, therefore:** <one sentence>
**Opening:** <situation, complication, question, answer>

| # | Title (the claim) | Job in the argument | Evidence it needs |
|---|---|---|---|

**Appendix:** <titles>
**Questions for you:** A1 ... A2 ... <the two or three that matter most first>
**Choices I made that you may want to change:** <two or three lines>
```

## Step 5: Render for the medium

`References/medium.md` has the full rules, the checklist of what a speaker supplies, and how to derive one rendering from the other. The short version:

| | Presented live | Read alone |
|---|---|---|
| Who sets the pace | The speaker | The reader |
| Title | Claim sentence, two lines at most | Claim sentence |
| Body | One visual and a few key terms the speaker also says | The visual with full sentences beside the part they explain |
| Explanation | In the speaker's voice and the notes | On the page |
| Detail | Appendix, pulled up on a question | Appendix, linked from the page |
| Position | Spoken signposts and a map that returns | A tracker on every page of a longer deck |
| Test | Does it make sense when lifted out of the deck? Does the voice have room? | Can someone who was never in the room follow it and act? |

When the same content must be both presented and read (a talk whose slides will be circulated), pick the primary medium, build that, and derive the other. With time for only one, build the read-alone version and present from a stripped copy: a reading deck can be talked over, and a sparse live deck leaves a reader with too little. Never count on a reader opening speaker notes. This is a different case from a pre-read followed by a meeting, which is two decks.

## Slides mode

Turn each title into a spec using the format and the slide-level rules in `References/slides.md`: the claim, the slide's job, what it shows and in what form, the words on the slide for this medium, what the speaker says or what the page must explain, what has to stay visible, and the source. Chart and diagram design go to `VisualizeReview`. The spec says what the visual must prove and names its form in plain words.

## Critique

**Before reading.** If the author gave no intake, infer the medium and purpose and say so. If they gave no statement of the deck's point, proceed, and make that the first question back.

Two passes, both best run in a fresh context that sees only the deck and the intake summary, because a reviewer who shares the author's context cannot see what the slides leave out. Use a subagent when the host allows it. Otherwise read deliberately as the named audience, and record in the note that the review ran in the same context.

**Pass 1, the cold audience.** Read only the titles in order and write the argument they make, the ask, and where it stops making sense. Then go through the whole deck in its medium: read each page alone for a pre-read, or imagine hearing it once at the speaker's pace for a live deck. List every term, chart or step that could not be followed from what the audience would have in front of them. If the restated point or ask differs from the intake, that is the top finding.

**Pass 2, the rubric.** Work through `References/rubric.md` in order. Each item gets PASS, FLAG, N/A or NOT SEEN, and every FLAG cites the slide. Then fold flags that describe one defect into a single finding that lists its check IDs, and rank the findings by what the audience would misunderstand or decide wrongly. Argument and sequence come before slide-level findings.

Look at slides as rendered whenever the visual matters. Extracted text shows what a slide says and hides what it shows. If `VisualizeReview` is installed, its extractor reads `.pptx` text and notes and its Intake file explains how to get a rendered view. When only an outline or a list of titles was provided, say so and mark the slide-level checks NOT SEEN.

**Verdict.** Ready: nothing would mislead the audience. Fix the slides: the claims and their order hold, and individual slides need work. Reorder: the claims exist and the sequence is wrong. Rebuild the storyline: the point cannot be restated from the deck, or the titles are not claims.

**Proposed storyline.** For Reorder and Rebuild, return a revised titles list beside the findings. The deck stays untouched. When the deck gives too little to write real claims, return claim frames with queries, such as "Churn rose because [A2: the cause the data supports]", and say that they are frames.

```markdown
**The deck as a cold audience took it:** <the argument from the titles, and the ask, one sentence each>. Matches your intent: yes / no (<what differs>) / unknown, no point was stated.
**Medium and purpose reviewed against:** <stated or inferred>
**How this was read:** <fresh context / same context as the author's material> · <rendered slides / extracted text / titles only>
**Verdict:** Ready / Fix the slides / Reorder / Rebuild the storyline. <confidence, in a plain sentence>
**What works:** <up to three specific strengths with slide numbers, or "nothing at this level yet">
**Top issues:** <the three findings that matter most>
**Not reviewed:** <for example whether the numbers are right, chart design, delivery>

| Rank | Checks | Slide | What it says or shows | Problem | Smallest fix |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**Passed:** <check IDs> · **N/A:** <check IDs> · **Not seen:** <check IDs>
**Proposed storyline:** <the revised titles list or claim frames, for Reorder and Rebuild>
**Questions for you:** A1 ... A2 ...
```

The table holds findings only. Rubric check IDs (F, S, Q, D, B, L, R, X) name checks, and author queries are numbered A1, A2 so the two never collide.

## Convert

Return a conversion as the storyline header and titles table for the new deck, then a spec block for each slide that changes.

- **Live to read-alone.** Keep the titles. For each slide, write on the page what the speaker would have said: how to read the chart, what follows from it, the definition of each term at first use. Add the summary page, the tracker, sources, links to the appendix, and what happens next. Check the eight items in `References/medium.md`.
- **Read-alone to live.** Keep the titles. Strip each page to its visual and a few key terms, and move the sentences to the notes. Split any page that holds more than one step. Add spoken signposts and the returning map. Decide where the room gets to talk.
- **Document to deck.** Do not paste the headings in as titles. Run Storyline on the document's argument. A document's order is usually the order the work was done, and a deck needs the order the audience needs.
- **After a pre-read.** The meeting deck is a different, short deck: the Discussion deck pattern. Its first slide is a one-page recap that also serves anyone who has not read, and carries anything new since the pre-read went out. Then the open questions and the decision, with the pre-read's pages as backup. Do not walk the room through what they have read.

## Honest persuasion

Story lowers an audience's scrutiny, which is the reason it works and the reason it carries a duty. The more a deck leans on narrative, the more it owes the audience an argument they can check. Hold every buy-in deck to these, and decline to help with the opposite:

1. Every claim the story rests on appears somewhere the audience can check it, with its source.
2. A single case stands for a stated base rate and never replaces it. Where no base rate exists, the slide says that the case is one case.
3. The real downside is stated plainly and is not buried among small ones.
4. Every option shown is one the author would accept being chosen. No decoy options.
5. Uncertainty is given as a range with what it rests on. Where no range can be defended, the slide says what is unknown.
6. The author would be comfortable saying the structure out loud: "I am opening with one customer's story because the numbers alone are easy to forget."

Checks 2 and 5 never license an invented number. A missing figure is a query to the author.

## Working together

The author owns the argument. Propose the spine and the storyline, and expect to revise them: the first version is how the author finds out what they think. When the author rejects a suggestion, do not argue it again in the same deck. Query facts and never supply them. Keep the author's terms of art and ask before renaming one.

Hand off at the seams. Words on the slides and in the notes go to `WritingPartner`. Charts, diagrams and layout go to `VisualizeReview`. The substance of a proposal goes to the review skill that fits it, such as `DeliveryReview` or `ResilienceReview`. A deck reviewed from several angles at once goes to `PanelReview`, where this skill's Critique sits at the structure layer.

## Rules the skill does not enforce

These are folklore or conventions with no test behind them, and enforcing them makes decks worse. Do not impose a slide count, the 10/20/30 rule, slides per minute, or 6x6 and 7x7 word limits. Do not design to a "10-minute attention span": the studies do not show one. Do not cite "seven plus or minus two" for bullets, "visuals are processed 60,000 times faster", "stories are 22 times more memorable", or the 7-38-55 rule. Do not force a hero's journey or a five-act arc on a business deck. Do not reframe a benefit as a loss because "losses loom larger", add a chart to look rigorous, or trim real options for fear of overload: each of those rests on findings that failed to replicate. Do not preach against decks or against bullets for lists that are lists. `References/evidence.md` has the sources.

## Gotchas

- **The order of the work is rarely the order for the audience.** Method, then data, then findings is how it was done. The audience needs the finding, then enough method to trust it.
- **Agenda slides are not maps.** A list of section names tells the audience nothing. A map shows how the parts of the argument or the system relate, and comes back with the current part marked.
- **A caveat that would change the decision belongs in the title.** The well-known briefing failures put the deciding fact in small type under a reassuring heading.
- **Experts need less scaffolding.** Definitions and step-by-step build-up help newcomers and slow experts. For a mixed room, put the scaffolding in the pre-read or the appendix.
- **Do not assume the pre-read was read.** Nobody has published how many people read one. Make the meeting's first slide a recap that works for someone who has not, or give reading time in the room.
- **First sight is a risk when it happens in the room.** If the meeting is the first time the deciders meet the idea, say so: practice assigns that job to conversations beforehand. A pre-read sent ahead is one way to do it.
- **Every slide gets lifted out.** Decks are reordered, forwarded and reused. Each slide carries its own claim, units, date and source.
- **Numbers fade and cases stick.** If the decision comes after the meeting, pair each deciding number with one concrete case.
- **These effects are small.** No structure guarantees agreement. The substance of the case and its fit to the audience outweigh any rule here.
