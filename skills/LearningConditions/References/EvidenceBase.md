# EvidenceBase — what this skill rests on, graded, with the ceiling stated

This skill was built from a full reading of one practitioner corpus (230 videos, 92.7 hours),
every substantive claim of which was then graded against the research record by six domain passes.
509 of 541 citations in that grading resolved against Crossref and NCBI before anything here was
written.

The full synthesis and per-domain verdicts are not published here. This file carries only what the
skill's behaviour depends on.

## The load-bearing findings

**Structure you build yourself beats structure you are given.** Creating a concept map outperforms
studying one at g = 0.72 against g = 0.43 (Schroeder et al. 2017). This generalises past mapping
and is the strongest single justification for why `Triage` routes so little to passive reference
and why `DriftCheck` tests decisions rather than artifacts.

**Comparison is unusually high-yield.** Studying two cases side by side beats studying them
separately at d = 0.50 across 57 experiments (Alfieri, Nokes-Malach and Schunn 2013). In one
applied test, separate study of the same cases was no better than no training at all.

**Hierarchical organisation beats detail-first, and beats rereading.** Eylon and Reif (1984) found
hierarchically organised presentation beat a detailed single-level version on recall, error
correction and the ability to modify what was learned, and beat a group who read the single-level
version twice. This is the controlled result underneath "organise before you accumulate."

**The isolated-versus-integrated distinction is real.** The expert-novice literature has made this
cut since Chi, Feltovich and Glaser (1981), with de Jong and Ferguson-Hessler (1986) sorting good
and poor problem solvers in the same cohort by deep structure versus surface features. The
practical consequence, which popular advice gets wrong, is that *understanding* can also be
isolated. Feeling that you understand does not establish integration.

**Cue-dependency limits what a flashcard can do.** Pan and Rickard (2018), 192 effect sizes,
N = 10,382, found transfer weakest of all to rearranged stimulus-response items, often showing no
positive transfer after correction. This is why `Triage` sends only arbitrary detail to cards, and
the evidence is stronger than the source material's own argument for the same conclusion.

**Diminishing returns are dose-scheduled, not person-dependent.** Rawson and Dunlosky (2011), 533
students and over 100,000 scored responses, found retrieval effects subadditive. Four separate
studies partitioned learners by ability and found flat moderation. The practical rule is to stop
early and space it, for everyone, rather than because you are strong.

**Implementation intentions replicate.** Specific if-this-then-that plans naming time and place
hold at roughly SMD 0.31 post-intervention and 0.24 at follow-up, without eroding over time. This
is the backbone of `Adherence` step 3, and it is notably one of the constructs that *survived* the
replication turbulence that took down several better-known ones.

**Errorful generation helps.** Guessing before learning is robust, with two qualifiers worth
carrying: near-misses beat wild guesses, and at least one well-powered study found nothing.

## The honest ceiling

- **There is no multiplier here.** No speed figure in the source corpus had a source, including
  the plausible-sounding ones, and this skill will not supply replacements. The honest promise is
  durable understanding and a practice that survives, not a rate.
- **Capacity instruments are coarse and slow.** `Instrument` can support better, same or worse over
  a quarter. It cannot support a magnitude, and building a score would reproduce the exact failure
  this project documented.
- **The drift signatures are a designed synthesis, not a validated instrument.** The underlying
  components (illusions of fluency, surface-versus-deep sorting, the illusion of explanatory
  depth) are each well evidenced. Their assembly into the five-signature check in `DriftCheck` is
  our construction. Say so if it is ever cited.
- **The adherence design is reasoned, not measured.** Sizing to the worst week, the counted
  minimum and the forgive-do-not-accumulate rule follow from the structure of the failure. Only
  the implementation-intention component carries direct evidence.

## The debunk list — do not spend time here

**Do not intervene on mindset to change behaviour.** Self-beliefs predict later achievement at
about β = .08. The intervention literature is genuinely contested with effect size running
inversely to study quality: the highest-quality meta-analysis found d = 0.05, non-significant after
publication-bias correction, and d = 0.02 in its best-evidence subset, while a lower-tier venue
reports 0.45. Authors with a financial incentive were 2.5 times likelier to report positive
effects, and higher-quality studies were *less* likely to show benefit. A measurement problem
compounds it: fixed and growth mindset are not mutually exclusive, correlating around .63 to .65
even after reverse-scoring, so instruments that sort people into "mindset-limited" are invalid
before prevalence is even asked. The defensible residue is mindset as a moderator of whether
someone sticks with training, which is why `Adherence` treats design as the lever instead.

**Do not invoke ego depletion or decision fatigue.** Two of the three largest multi-lab tests
returned d = 0.04 and d = 0.06, with data four times likelier under the null in one. Planning
rituals still work, via implementation intentions.

**Do not use the Zeigarnik effect as a memory claim.** A 2025 meta-analysis found no memory
advantage for unfinished tasks, alongside a real general tendency to resume them. Stopping
mid-task is a fine technique under the correct name, and where the recall advantage appears at all
it holds only for people already high in hope of success.

**Do not accept the unfalsifiable difficulty loop.** "Difficulty means it is working," combined
with "mistakes are the accelerant" and "slow progress means insufficient pressure," removes every
channel by which a learner could detect a bad method. Keep the asymmetry instead: low felt effort
reliably indicates ineffective study, high felt effort does not indicate effective study.

**Do not repeat the mechanism claim that transcription bypasses frontal meaning-making.** It is
contradicted, including by the anatomy of the region it names. The practice of writing fewer notes
survives on other grounds, and handwriting over typing is a small g = 0.144 whose preregistered
replication reproduced a difference in notes but not in learning.

**Do not police word count.** Higher word count predicted *better* performance in both the
original note-taking study and its replication, against the popular advice.
