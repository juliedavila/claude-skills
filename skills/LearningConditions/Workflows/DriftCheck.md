# DriftCheck — catch the failure that feels like success

**Gate:** the person produces, cold and without looking, the *reasoning* behind an artifact they
recently made. Not the artifact. The reasoning.

## The failure this exists to catch

There is a kind of learner for whom a method keeps failing while **feeling fine**. They have
unconsciously reverted to shortcuts that preserve the sensation of fluency: grouping things by
surface similarity rather than by cause, copying a structure rather than deciding it, producing
a clean artifact that encodes nothing.

This is the most dangerous failure mode in the entire source corpus of 230 videos, and it is
named exactly once. Every other video would score such a learner as succeeding, because the
visible output satisfies every stated criterion. The map looks right. The notes look right. The
session felt productive.

Drift is not a character failure and should never be framed as one. It is what practice does
under load: it optimises toward the version that feels the same and costs less. Expect it, check
for it on a schedule, and repair it without ceremony.

## Steps

### 1. Choose a recent artifact, at random rather than by choice

Take something made in the last two weeks. Do not let the person select it, because selection
finds the best one and the best one is not the question. Random selection is the whole method.

### 2. Close the source, and ask for the reasoning

The prompt is always about the *decisions*, never the content:

> "Source closed. Why is this item grouped with that one? Not what they have in common. What
> causes them to belong together, and what would have to be true for them to belong apart?"

Other forms, depending on the artifact:

- "Why is this at the top of the structure and that lower down? What breaks if you swap them?"
- "Which two of these are most easily confused, and what feature tells them apart?"
- "What did you decide to leave out, and why?"

### 3. Score against the drift signatures

**Surface grouping.** They group by shared words, shared topic, visual proximity, or the order
they encountered things. The tell is an answer like "they seemed related" or "they were in the
same chapter." Grouping by surface proximity is a memorisation aid wearing the costume of
conceptual work, and it is the single clearest drift signature.

**Copied structure.** They can state the structure but not defend the alternative that was
rejected. If nothing was rejected, nothing was decided, and a structure that was received rather
than chosen does almost none of the work that makes structuring valuable.

**No omissions.** Everything was included. Deciding what does not matter is most of the
cognitive work, so an artifact with nothing left out is usually transcription with better
formatting.

**Fluent nothing.** The explanation is smooth, confident, and contains no mechanism. Watch for
"and then it just works," borrowed jargon standing in for a step, and any account that would
satisfy someone who already understood it while teaching nothing to someone who did not.

### 4. Repair at the decision, not at the artifact

If drift is found, do not rebuild the artifact. Redo the *decision* that was skipped: make the
grouping call again out loud, with the reason, and name the alternative rejected. Then let the
artifact be whatever it needs to be afterward.

Rebuilding a prettier artifact is itself a drift behaviour. It feels like remediation and
changes nothing.

### 5. Set the next check

Fortnightly is a reasonable default while a practice is young, monthly once it is established.
Put it on the calendar now, because a drift check that depends on suspecting drift will never
run. The whole premise is that drift does not feel like anything.

## Output

Which artifact was tested, which signatures appeared, what the repaired decision was, and the
next check date.

## Gotchas

- **A confidence drop here is the workflow working.** The point is to break a fluency illusion,
  and that feels bad by design. Do not soften it, and do not reassure. Naming the drop as the
  expected and correct outcome is the supportive move; telling them it was probably fine is not.
- **Do not accept the artifact as evidence.** "But look, it's all there" is precisely the claim
  under test. The artifact looking right is the failure mode, not a defence against it.
- **Never let the person pick the artifact.** Selection defeats random sampling, and the
  instinct to pick a good one is itself worth naming when it appears.
- **Do not run this as a quiz on content.** Content recall is `Internalize`'s cold-recall gate
  and it belongs there. This workflow tests whether the *thinking* is still happening, which is
  a different question with a different failure mode.
- **Drift after a period of high load is expected, not diagnostic of the person.** Frame it as
  what practice does under pressure. A shame frame reliably produces avoidance of the next
  check, which removes the only instrument that works.
